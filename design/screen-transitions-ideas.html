HYBRID – Motion – Ideas, not yet built

Screens should move, not blink.

Today every navigation in HYBRID is the same 240–260 ms fade-and-rise, on both clients. It's clean, it's reduced-motion-safe, and it tells you nothing — a tab switch, a drill-down and a modal all look identical, and on web the outgoing screen simply vanishes. This is the case for a real transition system: motion that encodes hierarchy, carries the thing you tapped into the screen it opens, and can be grabbed mid-flight.

The reference

What "iOS 26" actually means

Stripped of the marketing, Apple's current transition language is five concrete mechanics. Each one is buildable on both of our clients — they're not magic, and three of them we're already half-way to.

Zoom navigation

The thing you tapped becomes the screen

A tapped card expands into the destination, corner radius unwinding to the screen edge, while the list behind scales back and dims. Going back reverses it into the same card. This is the single biggest perceived-quality win available.

Glass morph

Controls merge and split as fluid

Glass elements in a shared container flow into one another rather than cross-fading. Our nav lens already does the persisting-view version of this, including the stretch-on-travel.

Interruptible

Every transition tracks the finger

Nothing is a fire-and-forget animation. You can grab a transition mid-flight, reverse it, and release; springs resolve from wherever they are. This is what makes iOS feel alive rather than scripted.

Depth semantics

Direction encodes hierarchy

Sideways means sibling. Zoom-forward means deeper. Up-from-bottom with the parent receding means modal. You learn the app's shape without reading a single label.

Continuous chrome

Titles compress, they don't swap

Large titles shrink into the header as a function of scroll offset, not as a stepped state change. Same principle as our nav's scroll-collapse, applied to the masthead.

Material response

Glass reacts to movement

Specular highlights shift and blur radius modulates as content moves behind glass. A cheap approximation — modulating blur and rim opacity during a transition — gets most of the effect.

Wave 2 – the headline feature

Continuity

This is the wave that makes the app feel like iOS 26 rather than like a well-animated website. It's also where the real engineering sits: continuity requires measuring an element in one screen and re-projecting it in the next.

Interruptible back, with a live preview

Not started

Material

Wave 3 – no demo needed

Ambient field continuity

The gradient blob field (lg-field on web, AuroraField on mobile) already renders outside the screen wrapper — so it survives a screen change untouched. Give it a slow counter-drift during transitions and it becomes the fixed world the screens move through. Nearly free, and it's the detail that makes a transition feel spatial.

Wave 3 – no demo needed

Haptics on commit, not on touch

The nav already fires expo-haptics. The rule worth adopting everywhere: fire on the frame the transition commits, not when the finger lands. On an interruptible gesture that means at the threshold crossing, so the phone confirms the decision you just made.

Wave 3 – no demo needed

Focus mode for the live workout

Entering the logger already hides the nav pill (HIDE_ON in global-nav.tsx). Make the entry itself a statement: the world desaturates and blurs out over 400 ms, the logger rises through it. Leaving reverses. It marks "you are in a session" better than any banner.

Wave 3 – no demo needed

Celebration handoff

The PR celebration keyframes already exist — prTrophyPop, prSweep, prConfetti in globals.css:472. Wire them to the transition into the Done screen so the badge flies from the set row into the summary, instead of the summary playing its own animation after arriving.

The system

Motion tokens

The proposed contents of packages/core/src/motion.ts, consumed by globals.css as custom properties and by mobile as Easing / spring configs. Four durations and four curves cover everything in this document; anything that needs a fifth is probably wrong.

TokenValueCurveUsed by
duration.fast160 mse-exitExits, dismissals, press states
duration.base260 mse-standardSibling slides, entrances, crossfades
duration.zoom440 mse-zoomShared-element zoom, hero number travel
duration.sheet380 mse-springSheets, modals, the parent recede
ease.standardcubic-bezier(.2,.7,.3,1)—The default. Already the app's curve.
ease.zoomcubic-bezier(.2,.85,.25,1)—Long decelerations that must not feel slow
ease.exitcubic-bezier(.4,0,.9,.4)—Accelerating — things leave faster than they arrive
ease.springcubic-bezier(.34,1.4,.5,1)—Slight overshoot on arrival

stagger.step24 ms—Per-item delay, capped at 6 items
parallax.under0.33—How far the underlying screen moves
recede.scale0.92—Parent scale when presenting a modal

Feasibility

What's already in the box

Encouraging news: nothing in this document requires a dependency the repo doesn't already have, and the two heaviest items are unlocked by packages that are already installed.

NeedStatusDetail
Mobile shared elements Present react-native-reanimated 4.4.0 and react-native-worklets are already resolvable at the workspace root, and babel-preset-expo wires the plugin automatically. One catch: they are transitive, not declared in apps/mobile/package.json — add them as explicit dependencies before relying on them, or a hoisting change silently breaks the build.
Native stack animations Present react-native-screens 4.25.2 renders per-route animation options natively. Today only two Stack.Screen entries set any options at all.
Native Liquid Glass Present @expo/ui 56.0.18 already gives real SwiftUI glassEffect, Animation.spring and .animation(value:) — and the nav already uses all three.
Web shared elements Native platform The View Transitions API needs no library. next 16.2.7 + react 19.2 additionally expose <ViewTransition>. No animation library needs adding to web.
Web exit animations Blocked by structure The shell renders one keyed <div>, so the outgoing tree unmounts before it can animate. Either keep both trees alive for one frame, or let View Transitions snapshot the old one — the latter is much less invasive.
Verification Sandbox limit Native motion can't be verified here — no Xcode or simulator. Anything native needs a TestFlight build to sign off, the same constraint already recorded against swiftui-kit.

Guardrails

Rules and risks

  • Reduced motion is not negotiable. Every item above must resolve to its end state instantly when Reduce Motion is on. The app already does this correctly on both clients — the risk is a new transition bypassing the existing guard, so the tokens file should expose a single useMotion() hook that already returns zeroed durations.
  • Nothing over 450 ms. The zoom at 440 ms is the ceiling and it earns it by moving a large distance. A 400 ms fade is just slow.
  • A transition may never gate input. Taps during an animation queue to the destination; they don't get swallowed. Stagger in particular must never delay a tappable row past ~150 ms.
  • Don't stack effects. Zoom plus blur plus a scale-back plus a stagger, all at once, causes motion sickness in a meaningful share of users. One primary motion per transition, everything else supporting it quietly.
  • Back is the exact inverse of forward. If back doesn't reverse the transition it came from, the spatial model breaks and the motion becomes noise.
  • Parity by construction. Per the project rule, every item here ships on both clients in the same change — which is why the token file comes first, before any of the transitions that consume it.
  • Watch the JS-driver decision. useEntrance() deliberately uses the JS driver to avoid a Fabric race that stranded screens invisible. Any rewrite onto Reanimated must be verified on a real device against that exact failure, not assumed fixed.
  • Web has a code-splitting interaction. The shell lazy-loads nearly every screen. A screen that arrives mid-transition will pop unless the transition waits on the chunk — worth pairing with the already-planned web-code-splitting work.

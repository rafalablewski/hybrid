History — Timeline cards

Say it once. Ten ways to get there.

The timeline card currently narrates its data three times: the title, a metric chip, and then the raw block lines — so “Tennis, 75 min” appears twice, a swim states its time in the chip and again in the block line (in different units), and a gym session dumps every set of every exercise onto the shelf. Jony Ive's test applies directly: simplicity isn't the absence of clutter — it's describing purpose with nothing left to take away. Every idea below shows the same three sessions from the screenshot, so they compare honestly.

One fact, one place

A number appears once per card. If the chip says it, the body may not.

Detail is a tap deep

The card is a handle, not the record. Sets, splits and paces live on the session page.

Grammar per discipline

Lifting speaks in tonnage and top sets; cardio in distance and pace; sport in time. Never the same sentence.

The exceptional speaks

PRs and standout days earn ink. Ordinary days recede — that contrast is the design.

Diagnosis

Where the redundancy comes from

One component, SessionCard, renders title + chip + up to three raw blockSummary lines for every session shape. Quick sport logs are a single block named after the sport — so the card repeats itself by construction.

Flaw 1 — echoed title

Single-block sport sessions print the sport as the title and as the block name.

Flaw 2 — echoed metric

The chip and the block line state the same duration, minutes apart on the same card.

Flaw 3 — unit drift

“0.6 km” in the chip, “612 m” in the body. Same fact, two dialects — reads as a bug.

Flaw 4 — the data dump

Lifting cards enumerate every set of every exercise. That's the session page's job, not the shelf's.

Deduplicate, nothing else

Idea 01
The smallest true fix — subtract, don't redesign.

Three rules, no new visual language: a block whose name matches the title is never listed; a fact stated in the chip is struck from the body (the swim keeps only its pace); set enumerations collapse to a set count. One unit system per metric — metres in both places or kilometres in both, never each.

Tennis becomes a one-row card. This is Ive's discipline at its plainest: the confidence to remove.

Gain

Ships in an afternoon; zero relearning; parity is one shared engine change.

Cost

Lifting cards still list exercises without saying anything interesting about them.

The headline number

Idea 02
Every session has one number that mattered. Print that.

Invert the hierarchy: the key metric becomes the display element — large, light-weight, tabular — and the title drops to a mono sub-line. Lifting leads with tonnage, cardio with distance, sport with time. Chips disappear entirely; the PR count joins the sub-line in lime.

The timeline stops being a list of labels and becomes a column of numbers you can read like an instrument — scanning down the rail you literally watch your week add up.

Gain

Instantly scannable; strong identity; numbers align down the column.

Cost

Custom titles (“Morning workout”) lose prominence; two same-sport days look near-identical.

The shape of the work

Idea 03
“90×7, 70×8, 100×5…” becomes a form you read in 200 ms.

The set enumeration was information trying to be a picture. Let it be one: a per-set load sparkline — bar height is load, lime marks top sets, teal marks a second exercise. The wave-loading pattern of that deadlift session is visible now, which the string of numerals never achieved.

One mono line beneath names the lifts and their single best set. Honest materials: the data itself becomes the ornament, so the card needs no other decoration.

Gain

Session character (waves, pyramids, straight sets) readable at a glance; dense yet calm.

Cost

A new visual element to build well on both clients; meaningless for bodyweight-only sets.

Best set only

Idea 04
Each lift gets one line: its name and the set worth remembering.

Keep the exercise list — it's what distinguishes “Morning workout” from “Afternoon workout” — but each line carries only the top set (highest load × its reps), with a lime PR mark where that set broke a record. The card answers the question you actually have on the shelf: what did I pull, and was it a record?

Sport and cardio sessions drop their block lines entirely; the chip already says everything.

Gain

Every line now earns its ink; PRs land on the exact lift, not just a count.

Cost

Volume-focused athletes lose the sets-completed picture on the shelf.

One sentence

Idea 05
The card body is a single composed sentence, not a data readout.

Retire chips and block lines both. A per-shape sentence grammar in packages/core composes one line: cardio speaks distance-time-pace, lifting speaks tonnage-lifts-PRs, sport speaks time. Because it's prose, nothing can repeat — a sentence that said “75 minutes” twice would read as broken, which is exactly the guarantee we want from the layout.

Gain

Warmest, most human treatment; structurally immune to redundancy; trivially translatable.

Cost

Slower to scan than aligned figures; grammar needs care in every locale.

The spine speaks

Idea 06
Move meaning into the rail; flatten the cards into rows.

The timeline dot stops being punctuation and becomes the datum: colour is discipline (lime lifting, teal sport/cardio — already the app's code), size is session load. Cards flatten to single rows — title and one metric — because the spine now carries the at-a-glance story. Twice the history fits on one screen.

Deference in Ive's sense: the chrome (cards, borders, chips) withdraws so the structure itself is the display.

Gain

2× density; the month's rhythm — heavy days, gaps, streaks — visible as a shape.

Cost

No exercise detail on the shelf at all; dot-size semantics need to be learnable.

Quiet unless exceptional

Idea 07
Ordinary days are one row. Record days get to talk.

Hierarchy by merit: every card defaults to a single row — title plus one metric. A session only expands when something happened worth expanding for: a PR names the exact lift and set in lime, a standout volume day states its total. The lime border whispers before you even read.

This makes the timeline emotionally honest — scrolling your month, the days that mattered rise to meet you, and the rest keep respectfully out of the way.

Gain

Strong reward loop for PRs; most days compress to near-nothing.

Cost

Needs a well-tuned “exceptional” threshold or every card ends up quiet.

One grammar

Idea 08
Exactly two lines, always: the name, then one mono stat line.

The strictest system of the ten: every card is title + one stat line, full stop. The line is composed per shape from a fixed grammar — lifting: tonnage – lifts – time – PRs; cardio: distance – time – pace; sport: time. Leading figure emphasised, en-dash separated, tabular numerals so the column aligns down the shelf.

Chips retire (the stat line replaced them), so the top-right corner returns to silence. Uniformity here isn't blandness — it's the grid that makes the numbers comparable.

Gain

Perfectly predictable rhythm; redundancy is grammatically impossible; easiest to test.

Cost

No exercise names on the shelf; single-metric sport cards feel sparse.

The day is the card

Idea 09
Group by day, not by session — the unit you actually think in.

Hybrid athletes double up constantly (the screenshot's Saturday has squash and a lift). Make the day the card: a mono date header with the day's own summary, sessions as rows inside it. The rail no longer repeats a date per session, two-session days shrink to one surface, and a “what did Saturday cost me” read appears for free.

Gain

Matches the mental model; day-level totals; less chrome per screen.

Cost

Individual sessions lose their own tap target size; a bigger structural change to both clients.

The ledger

Idea 10
No cards at all. A training log as an honest instrument.

The most radical reduction: retire the card metaphor and set history as a ledger — hairline rows, mono date, display-face title, one right-aligned tabular metric. Discipline reads through colour alone. Seven days fit where three cards sat; the whole month becomes one legible column of work done.

This is the “aluminium and glass” answer — no upholstery, just the material. It would live best as a fourth view alongside Agenda / Weeks / Timeline rather than replacing them.

Gain

Maximum density and calm; beautiful with months of data; near-zero chrome to maintain.

Cost

Small tap targets; least expressive for PR celebration; a departure from the app's card language.

Recommendation

What I'd ship

Idea 01 is the floor — the deduplication rules are correct under every other concept, so land them first regardless of direction. Then compose 08 + 04 + 07: the one-line stat grammar as every card's base, best-set exercise lines added only for lifting, and the quiet/exceptional hierarchy deciding when a card gets to say more than two lines. Those three agree with each other and stay recognisably AURORA.

03 (sparkline) is the one worth prototyping as a delight layer on lifting cards. 06, 09 and 10 are structural swings — good candidates for evolving the existing Agenda / Weeks / Timeline switcher rather than changes to the default card.

All composition logic belongs in packages/core (engines/history-views.ts, engines/session.ts) so web and mobile render one source of truth — per the parity rule. Same three sessions used in every mock: Swimming 612 m / 31 min, Tennis 75 min, Morning workout 7.4 t / 3 PR.

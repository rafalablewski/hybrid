HYBRID  /  Recovery system  /  Specification & build record

Heat as a
recovery input

Sauna is the most common recovery practice HYBRID cannot see, and the only one no device will ever report. It is two typed numbers — minutes, and how hot it was. This is the complete specification for making those two numbers count: what they are allowed to move, how the arithmetic works, how an athlete records them, and the order it was built in. Every phase is built; the measurement layer in §5 waits only on the athlete's own data, not on code.

01 — The gap

The gap this closed

Before this change, grep -ri sauna across the whole repository returned nothing. Not a session kind, not a signal, not a check-in field, not a string. An athlete who sits in a sauna four times a week was doing something that plausibly changes how much training they can absorb, and every engine in HYBRID was blind to it.

That is the gap. What follows is deliberately narrower than “add sauna tracking”, because the interesting question is not how to store fifteen minutes — it is what those fifteen minutes are allowed to move, and on what evidence.

No device will ever measure this. A watch does not know you sat in a sauna, and nothing in the room reports to us. The entire input is what the athlete types: how long, and how hot. That is not a weakness to design around — it is the fact that settles the architecture in §3, and it is why temperature is a first-class field here rather than a nice-to-have deferred to a later release.

02 — What the evidence supports

Two effects, on two timescales, with very different support

ChannelWhat the research shows StrengthWhere it lands in HYBRID
Chronic
weeks		 Heat acclimation: plasma volume expansion of roughly 3–5% over 7–10 days of consistent exposure, raised HSP70, improved thermoregulation and cardiovascular stability at submaximal loads. Good. Repeated trials, consistent direction, and a cohort dose-response that strengthens at 4+ sessions / week. The MRV recovery multiplier — how much work you can absorb.
Acute
hours		 Faster strength recovery or reduced soreness after a single session: thin and mixed. Improved perceived neuromuscular recovery in some trials, nothing over passive rest in others. Weak. Largely perceptual and parasympathetic rather than tissue repair. A small, capped, subordinate readiness credit.
Cost
same night		 Sauna is itself a load: heart rate 100–150 bpm, real fluid loss, and suppressed overnight HRV when taken hot, long, or immediately after a hard session. Well established, and it fires in exactly the case an athlete is most likely to log. Nothing. The wearable already measures it — see §3.

Sources behind the chronic row: Scoon 2007; Stanley 2015; Zurawlew 2016 for the hot-water-immersion analogue; Laukkanen’s Finnish KIHD cohort for the dose-response, which is observational. Behind the acute row: Mero 2015 (far-infrared, perceived recovery) against several null results. Every constant proposed below is a prior drawn from this literature, not a fitted value — §5 is how we replace them with measurements.

03 — The decision that shapes everything

Sauna is a prior. The wearable is a measurement.

If sauna improves sleep quality and parasympathetic tone, then biometricAdjustment() already credits it — at up to ±15 points, read from HRV, resting heart rate and sleep against the athlete’s own baseline. That is not a proxy for the effect. On the mornings it matters, it is the effect.

So a flat “+3 because you saunaed” counts one night of physiology twice. And on a bad dose — long, hot, straight after a threshold session — it credits the sauna while the wearable correctly debits it, and the athlete watches two terms on one card argue about one night.

The rule

A measurement beats a prior. When a fresh wearable reading exists, the heat credit is zero — and the card says so out loud rather than silently rounding to nothing. When there is no reading, the prior supplies a small, bounded stand-in.

This is not a new posture. It is the one the codebase already takes twice: BIOMETRIC_FRESH_DAYS exists because a months-old sync was pinning a permanent nudge onto a live score — no measurement, no adjustment — and landmark-adapt.ts already corrects population priors with observed response rather than stacking one on the other.

It also matters commercially: a large share of athletes have no wearable at all, and the biometric term drops out entirely after seven days without a reading. The prior fires most often for exactly the users the ±15 term cannot serve.

A typed number is allowed to count — in its own tier

HYBRID already runs on self-reports: RPE, the post-session feel, the daily check-in, every gram of logged food. None of those are measured either, and all of them move real numbers. So “the athlete typed it” is not a reason to discount heat.

But the app keeps a strict hierarchy, and this is where the device-truth rule points even though no device is involved: a measurement outranks a report. Heat is the one recovery input that will never graduate out of the report tier — which is precisely why the suppression rule above is the right permanent shape rather than a stopgap. It is not waiting for a sensor. There is never going to be one.

Why there is no negative term

The overdose case is real. It is also unmeasurable from a logged duration — and the wearable measures its consequence the following morning anyway. Putting an arc on the readiness ring for a cost the engine is guessing at would satisfy the sum law’s arithmetic while violating its point, which is that every drawn cause is one the score actually has. So it becomes guidance copy instead: log a sauna within an hour of a high-strain session and the sheet says what the literature says. It does not dock points.

04 — How it is counted

The arithmetic, in full

One new pure module, packages/core/src/engines/heat.ts, exporting one function. Every constant is exported so the admin Engine Room renders live values rather than a hand-copied duplicate — the same rule the other engines follow.

// engines/heat.ts — two typed numbers per sitting: minutes, tempC intensity(T, p) = clamp((T − floor[p]) / (ref[p] − floor[p]), 0, HEAT_INTENSITY_MAX) equivMin = Σ minutesᵢ × intensity(tempCᵢ, protocolᵢ) // sittings inside HEAT_WINDOW_H dose = HEAT_CREDIT_MAX × (1 − e^(−equivMin / HEAT_TAU_MIN)) decay = 0.5 ^ (hoursSinceLast / HEAT_HALF_LIFE_H) points = bio ? 0 : round(dose × decay) // the suppression rule, §3

Duration alone is not the dose. Twenty minutes at 90 °C and twenty minutes in a 55 °C infrared cabin are not the same stimulus, and a model that reads only the clock would score them identically. So temperature converts minutes into equivalent minutes at the reference, and everything downstream — the saturating curve, the cap, the decay — is unchanged and now takes an honest input.

And neither is duration-and-temperature. Air temperature is only a proxy for thermal strain, and the proxy has a different constant in each modality — so a single ramp calibrated on dry sauna scores a 45 °C steam room at exactly zero, because 45 °C is the dry floor. Each protocol therefore carries its own pair, and every pair answers the same question: what air temperature, in this modality, loads an athlete the way 80 °C of dry sauna does?

The temperature model — one ramp per modality

Sauna
45→80°C

The anchor. Dry air evaporates sweat freely, which is precisely why it has to be this hot to load you.

Steam

At  100% humidity evaporative cooling is blocked, so far less air temperature does the same work.

Infrared

3points

One fifth of the wearable’s ±15. A prior must not out-vote a measurement even when the measurement is missing.

HEAT_TAU_MIN

Saturating, so an hour is not twice a half hour. Ten minutes earns about half the cap.

HEAT_HALF_LIFE_H

Last night’s sauna is mostly spent by tonight. Same idiom as the fatigue engine’s two-day half-life.

HEAT_WINDOW_H

Still a calibration, not a measurement

Three ramps is better than one, and it is not the same as solved. A steam room at 60% humidity and one at 100% are genuinely different doses, and this still cannot tell them apart — it reads the modality you picked and the number on the wall, not the air you were actually in. The honest claim is that each protocol is now calibrated against the same anchor, not that thermal strain is being measured. The floor and reference pairs are judgement calls; they are exported constants the Engine Room renders live, so retuning any of them is one line.

The same 22 minutes in a 55 °C infrared cabin runs the identical arithmetic to a different answer: intensity 0.29, 6.3 equivalent minutes, dose 1.027, credit +1. That gap is the entire reason temperature is collected.

Had the watch synced at 06:12, every line above would still be computed and printed — and the last one would read +0  (suppressed: wearable read 06:12). That is the difference between a rule and a silent zero, and it is the reason the derivation surface is listed as a hard requirement in §7 rather than a nice-to-have.

The chronic channel — the MRV multiplier

The better-evidenced half lands somewhere that already has the right shape. landmark-profile.ts turns a population volume table into an estimate for one athlete through recovery multipliers, each carrying a LandmarkFactor with the value that produced it — so the screen can already answer “why is my ceiling 16 and not 20”. Heat becomes one more factor in that list.

Crucially it is derived from the signals, never asked. The athlete has already told us by logging; a second question in a profile form would be a worse copy of an answer we hold.

The frequency tier counts sittings rather than equivalent minutes, because sessions per week is what the cohort evidence is actually expressed in. But a sitting only counts once it clears HEAT_SESSION_MIN_EQUIV — 10 equivalent minutes, which is ten minutes at the reference or eight at 90 °C. Five minutes in a cool cabin is not a fourth session.

05 — Replacing the priors with measurements

The version worth having

Every constant in §4 comes from published research on other people. HYBRID already owns a better instrument for this athlete.

engines/recovery-pairs.ts measures each athlete’s own clearance rate by matching the immediate post-session read to a later recovery read, and it is rigorous about what it throws away: a second session inside the gap contaminates the pair, so the pair is dropped. Its own header states the standard — the clearance estimate is allowed to be slow to arrive; it is not allowed to be wrong.

Tag each pair with heat — true when a sauna signal falls between the session end and the recovery read — and the athlete’s own history splits in two.

// engines/recovery-pairs.ts
saunaClearance(sessions, recovery, signals) → {
  withHeat:    RecoveryIndex,   // ratio, confidence, pairs
  withoutHeat: RecoveryIndex,
  delta:       number,          // negative = clears faster after heat
  confidence:  number,          // zero until BOTH sides clear MIN_RECOVERY_PAIRS
}

Two pairs a side is the floor, so realistically this says nothing for four to six weeks. That is correct, and it is the same patience the existing estimator already demands. Once it clears the bar, HEAT_CREDIT_MAX stops being a literature constant for that athlete and becomes their measured delta — the same prior-corrected-by- observation move landmark-adapt.ts and calibrateRisk() already make elsewhere in the stack.

It is also the only version of this feature that can survive the question “how do you know?”, because the answer stops being a citation and becomes the athlete’s own log.

06 — How it is logged

Two Signals per sitting, and no migration

A Signal carries exactly one number, and a sitting is two. That is not an obstacle — it is a solved problem in this codebase. The food log already writes up to eight Signals for one logged food and reassembles them by grouping on (exact timestamp, source), with a composite id so deleting the entry removes every row it wrote. Heat is the same shape with two rows instead of eight.

// one save in the app → two rows sharing an EXACT ts
{ kind: "sauna",     value: 22, unit: "min", source: "manual", ts: "2026-08-11T21:40:00Z" }
{ kind: "saunaTemp", value: 90, unit: "C",   source: "manual", ts: "2026-08-11T21:40:00Z" }

// engines/signals.ts — the lines that gate it
type SignalKind = … | "sauna" | "saunaTemp"
META.sauna     = { unit: "min", better: "high" }
META.saunaTemp = { unit: "C",   better: "high" }

// engines/heat.ts — regrouped on read, exactly like derivedFoodEntries()
heatSittings(signals) → [{ minutes: 22, tempC: 90, ts, ids: ["sig_a", "sig_b"] }]

A sitting with no temperature row still counts, at the reference — an entry logged before the field existed, or one where the athlete genuinely did not know, is read as 80 °C and marked as assumed rather than silently treated as measured. The same distinction BiometricMetric.measured already draws. A temperature outside HEAT_TEMP_BOUNDS (40–120 °C) is rejected at the API, not clamped quietly: 900 is a typo, and a typo should bounce rather than become 120.

09 — Prerequisite

A live defect the signal stream already has

Found while scoping this. Independent of sauna, and it lands first.

Mobile’s fetchSignals() calls GET /api/signals with no kind filter, and that route returns the 500 newest rows across all kinds. Every logged food writes up to eight Signal rows — four macros plus the satFat / sugar / fiber / salt label panel.

Signal rows per logged foodup to 8
Five foods a day40 rows / day
Plus hydrationmore still
500-row window covers≈ 12 days

priorBaseline() then reads its HRV and sleep baseline out of that same truncated stream. So a diligent nutrition logger’s readiness baseline is being computed from a window the food log is quietly evicting, and the ±15 wearable term is degraded for exactly the athletes who use the app most.

The failure is silent: today’s reading still survives the seven-day freshness gate, so the term keeps printing — against a baseline built from fewer samples than it asks for. Fix by filtering server-side per kind for the biometric read, or paginating per kind. Any new signal kind, sauna included, joins the same contested stream and inherits the same unreliability.

Tracked as signals-read-window-crowding in the capabilities registry.

10 — How it gets built

Four phases, in this order

The order is not arbitrary. Phase 1 is what makes phase 4 possible, and phase 4 is the version worth having. All four phases and the prerequisite are built — the engine, all nine Engine Room touch points, the mobile sheet, the Today row, the post-session entry and the measurement layer, in EN, PL and DE. Phase 4 now waits only on the athlete's own data: it stays silent until both sides of the comparison clear the pair floor, which is four to six weeks of sittings and check-ins.

11 — Decisions

What I need from you before building

Suppression: hard or blended?

The recommendation is a hard zero whenever a fresh wearable reading exists — one sentence anyone can explain. The alternative blends the two by wearable confidence, which is defensible and produces a number nobody can describe in a sentence, and makes the Engine Room’s arithmetic much harder to read.

Traditional sauna only, or is steam and infrared in the mix?  ANSWERED — ALL THREE

So the protocol tag shipped, and it is not a footnote: with one ramp a 45 °C steam room scored exactly zero, because 45 °C is the dry sauna's floor. Each modality now has its own floor and reference, and the tag rides in Signal.source — still no migration, and "manual" alone stays a dry sauna, which is what every row written before this looks like.

Accepted input range  ANSWERED — 40 TO 120 °C

One flat gate across all three modalities, deliberately. It is a plausibility check against typos, not a claim about physiology — a steam room never runs at 110 °C, but rejecting that here would mean the API had opinions about modalities, and the per-protocol clamp already stops an implausible entry from earning an implausible dose.

Still a judgement call rather than a finding: the three floor/reference pairs themselves.

Scope of the first release

Recommendation: ship the prerequisite plus phases 1 and 2 together. That is logging both numbers, both entry points, and the best-evidenced engine effect, with no risk of double-counting and no readiness term to defend yet.

°C only, or °F as well?

Recommendation: store °C always, and derive the display unit from the weight unit the athlete already set (lb → °F), the way heightUnitFor() already derives inches against centimetres. No third preference, no ambiguous stored values.

Are 3 points and 4% the right ceilings?  DECIDED — KEPT

They stay. They are priors, not fitted values, chosen to be too small rather than too large — and with three calibrated ramps the intensity model now does more of the work, so raising the cap would be adding a second guess on top of a better first one. Phase 4 replaces the 3 with the athlete's own measured delta, which is a stronger answer than any number picked here.

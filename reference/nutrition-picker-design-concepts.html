Hybrid — Nutrition, add to meal

Six containers, one question.

The Snacks picker asks one thing — what did you eat? — and answers it with six containers of near-identical weight, two text fields that look alike, four tabs that hide three-quarters of your own food, and a screen that runs out of content at 40 % and leaves the rest black. Nothing on it is louder than anything else, so nothing on it is the subject.

Below: what the current screen is actually doing, then six concepts. Each takes one organizing idea all the way rather than tidying the six boxes, and each is drawn in the app's own vocabulary — Explore section heads, the rail-tail grammar, the sheet-pad rule, chartreuse reserved for go. Every one is specified for both clients; web and mobile ship together.

The screen today

Everything is a box, so nothing is a subject.

Reproduced at left, with each container outlined. The faults are not decoration errors — they are hierarchy errors. Six peers cannot rank themselves, and the athlete has to read the screen to work out which control is the one they want.

01

Six containers, no subject

Quick add, its caption, search, two buttons, the tab bar, the result. Same radius, same hairline, same fill. The eye has nowhere to land first.

02

Two fields that look the same

Quick add and search sit 90 px apart in the same shape with the same left-hand glyph. Two inputs stacked is the interface admitting it doesn't know which question it's asking.

03

The accent is spent on navigation

Chartreuse is the app's one go colour. Here the loudest chartreuse object is a tab — a statement about which list you're looking at — and the second is a 64 px add ring on a row. Nothing on screen is go.

04

A manual under a control

Type what you ate. Numbers, units and your own foods. A field that needs a caption to be understood is a field that isn't finished — and the caption is set in mono, which this app reserves for figures.

05

The screen has no floor

One result, then roughly 55 % black down to a tab bar floating alone. The picker knows dozens of foods this athlete eats and shows one.

06

Rare work priced like constant work

Create Food is used a handful of times ever and takes a third of the screen's top, at the same weight as logging, which happens every day.

07

The result breaks the column

The one row sits on a black card running wider than everything above it, with no hairline — a different material for no reason, at the exact moment the screen finally has something to say.

Concept 01 — One Field

One question, one field.

The grammar and the database are not two features. They are two answers to the same sentence.

Quick add already parses what you ate; search already looks it up. Splitting them into two identical boxes makes the athlete choose a mechanism before they've said a word. Here there is one field. You type, and every source answers underneath it in one ranked list: what the grammar understood first — the row you tap to approve, exactly as quick-add already works — then your own foods, then the world under a section head.

The barcode stays where it already is, as the field's trailing glyph, because scanning is the same question asked with a camera. The four tabs are gone: sources become section heads inside one list, which is how Explore has always ranked things. At rest — nothing typed — the list is your recents, so the screen has a floor before you touch it.

Quick Log and Create Food fall to the tail of the list as bare + affordances. Per the exit rule, no ring: they grow the library in place, they don't go anywhere.

Concept 02 — What's Left

The subject is the gap, not the box.

Nobody opens this screen to search. They open it because the day still owes them something.

So the screen states that first, in the app's display-weight mono, the way the Builder's One Number readout does: 612 kcal left, 48 g protein. Everything below is measured against it. Each row carries a delta — what it does to the gap — right-aligned in a tabular column, and the list is ordered by what closes this best, not by what you happened to log last March.

This is the concept that finally earns the accent. The chartreuse figure at the top is the one number on screen, it moves when you log, and the rings that move it are the only other accented things. Log something and watch the header fall — the screen answers, rather than waiting to be searched.

It also makes over-logging visible: a row that would push protein past target reads its delta in sand, not chartreuse, and the bar carries past the tick. No warning copy needed.

Concept 03 — The Ledger

Delete every container.

A list of foods does not need boxes to be a list of foods.

The most restrained of the six, and the one closest to what the rest of the app already does well. No cards, no pill bar, no tinted tab, no fill of any kind: Explore-standard section heads (bold display title left, mono meta right), hairline rows, and one right-aligned tabular column of figures running the full height of the screen. Whitespace does the grouping that six borders were doing badly.

Because the figures line up in a real column, the screen becomes readable as a table — you can compare 133 against 63 against 190 at a glance, which is the actual question when you're standing in a kitchen at 21:12. The single field sits above, unadorned, and the whole screen is legible in one look.

It is also the cheapest to build: it deletes code rather than adding it, and it is the natural home for the sources that already exist — a verified source is a mark beside the name, not another badge in another box.

Concept 04 — 21:12

The app knows the hour. Use it.

You do not eat a database at 21:12. You eat one of four things, and the app has watched you do it for months.

The screenshot is taken at 21:12 with the meal already set to Snacks, and the screen still opens as a blank search. This concept opens instead on what this athlete actually eats at this hour: a short list of large subjects, each carrying the portion it was last logged at, each one tap. No portion sheet for a food whose portion has never varied — that is a question the app already knows the answer to.

Search doesn't disappear; it stops being the frame. It's a glyph in the header that pushes the search state in over the top, which is the honest ranking: the fallback for a food you've never logged sits behind one tap, and the thing you do every night sits under your thumb.

The typographic move that makes it work: those rows are set at 18/700, bigger than any row elsewhere in the app. They are the content, not a list of candidates. And the dead 55 % becomes the screen's actual subject.

Concept 05 — The Tray

A snack is rarely one food.

The current screen commits on every tap and throws you back to the diary. Three foods means the same journey three times.

So let the picker accumulate. Tapping adds to a tray that sums live at the foot of the screen, and one commit writes the lot. The tray is the only filled surface on the screen, which is exactly right — it is the only thing holding a decision that hasn't happened yet.

It matches how the builder already treats a routine: compose, watch the number grow, save once. And it makes a mistake cheap — a wrong tap is a chip you flick off, not a diary entry you have to go and find.

The tray obeys the sheet rule exactly: its pad is sheetPadBottom(insetBottom), one number, a maximum and never a sum, and nothing inside it trails a pad of its own. It covers the tab bar rather than reserving clearance for it.

Concept 06 — The Shelf

Stop hiding three-quarters of the athlete's own food.

Four exclusive tabs mean that at any moment, 75 % of what this athlete has ever saved is one tap away and invisible.

Tabs are the right control when the lists are alternatives. These aren't — recents, favourites, saved meals and your own foods are all yours, and the answer is usually in whichever one you're not looking at. So they become shelves: one full-bleed rail per source, all on screen, scrolling under the physical edge exactly as the exercise-widget rail does — negative margins the width of the gutter, matching internal padding, so resting cards still align with the content column.

Each rail ends in the shared rail tail, and the grammar is the one the rules already fix: a ringed arrow leaves — it opens that source as a full screen. It is never a bordered box, because a filled card at the end of a row of foods reads as one more food that turned out to be empty.

This is also the only concept that scales visibly with the athlete: the shelves get longer as the library grows, which is a quietly good feeling and something the tab bar could never express.

If you only build one thing

01 and 02 are the spine. The rest bolt on.

They are not six alternatives so much as one screen at six depths. One Field fixes the hierarchy, What's Left gives the screen a subject, and the other four are refinements that each survive on their own.

Build first

01 One Field + 02 What's Left

One input, the gap as the header, one ranked list beneath. This alone deletes five containers, returns the accent to go, and gives the screen a floor.

Then

04 The Hour, as the resting state

What the list shows before you type. Pure gain — no new surface, no new schema, and it turns the empty 55 % into the fastest path in the app.

Then

05 The Tray, when logging is plural

Worth it the moment the diary shows more than one entry per meal, which it already does most evenings.

Style, not structure

03 The Ledger and 06 The Shelf

Two answers to the same question — how the list is drawn. The Ledger is the quieter and cheaper one; the Shelf is the one that shows the library growing. Pick one, don't ship both.

What dies in all six
  • ✕The second text field. One question gets one input.
  • ✕The four-tab pill bar — and with it, chartreuse spent on navigation state.
  • ✕The instructional mono caption. The placeholder carries it, or the field is wrong.
  • ✕Card chrome on Quick Log and Create Food. They become bare + tails — they grow the library, they don't go anywhere.
  • ✕The black full-bleed result card. Rows sit in the content column on a hairline, like every other list in the app.
  • ✕The doubled chevron in the header. Back is ‹; the meal switcher keeps the ⌄.

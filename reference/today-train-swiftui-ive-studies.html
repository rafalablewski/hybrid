Not the glass. The view set.

what "SwiftUI" actually buys us

Liquid Glass is a material, and a material is the least interesting thing SwiftUI has to offer. It changes how a surface looks; it doesn't change what a surface knows. Chasing it was the wrong read of the brief, so it's demoted here to the one place it earns its keep — a control that genuinely floats over scrolling content.

What's actually worth inheriting is the view set, and the installed @expo/ui@56 exposes almost all of it. These are not approximations: each one is a real SwiftUI view rendered by the system, arriving with its own separators, insets, selection behaviour, Dynamic Type response, VoiceOver semantics and — the part no hand-drawn React Native view ever gets for free — its own future. When Apple revises the list row in iOS 27, a List follows. A View with a borderRadius does not.

Nine of these are load-bearing in the screens that follow, and the card currently uses none of them.

The one exception The Train/Analyse/Social switcher keeps its glass lens. It is a control that floats over moving content, which is the single condition the material was designed for — and it already ships in liquid-seg.tsx. Everywhere else, structure beats material.

The card is honest and it works. What it isn't, is native. Every element is drawn by hand: the border, the shadow, the three full-bleed hairlines, the seven flex chips, the dashed square, the two competing pills. Nothing here is a control the system owns, so nothing inherits the system's structure, motion or accessibility.

Count what the eye passes before it reaches a fact: Today → Train → Your training → Last 7 days → Nothing logged yet. Five headings, and the first four are all names for the same thing.

The specific charges
  • 01A drawn box and a shadow and a border and three hairlines — four separation devices where Section uses one.
  • 02The week is a fixed Wed→Tue window that can't be scrolled, so a caption has to explain the control's own limits.
  • 03Two actions at the same weight in two different vocabularies. Both belong here — only one can be first.
  • 04🎾 as content. Emoji don't tint, don't take weight, don't answer Dynamic Type, and Apple redraws them without asking.
  • 05The tab bar smears "Train your way" into a ghost — a hand-rolled blur doing a job the scroll edge effect does properly.
  • 06Changing day remounts on key={sel.dateKey}. Values hard-cut. Nothing in the card animates at all.
  • 07A past day can't be edited or backfilled from here, and there is no way to reach tomorrow.

The border, the drop shadow and the solid ink2 fill are three ways of saying "this is a surface". Section inside a List with listStyle('insetGrouped') says it once, in the system's own voice — and hands over row insets, separator rules, header/footer slots, selection and swipe actions as part of the bargain.

Then fix the corners. iOS 26 made concentricity a rule rather than a taste: a nested radius equals its parent's minus the padding between them, so every corner shares one centre. Today the card is 28 and the tile inside it is 12, drawn on unrelated arcs — which is why the tile reads as pasted on rather than set in.

Both actions are in this frame, and in every frame after it. That is the rule the rest of the study is built on, not a detail of one state.

  • +ASection in kit.tsx wrapping the native List/Section, with the RN plate as the off-iOS floor.
  • +RADIUS.concentric(parent, pad) — one helper, every nested radius derived.
  • −Two of three hairlines. done-floor.tsx:86 becomes whitespace; the week/day seam stays.

Seven flexed chips can only ever show seven days, which is why the header carries a LAST 7 DAYS caption explaining the control's limits. A horizontal ScrollView under scrollTargetBehavior('viewAligned') and scrollTargetLayout makes history simply there, to the left, as far back as the account goes — and the caption evaporates. Its slot in the header goes to the exit into History.

The selection travels on matchedGeometryEffect, not a repaint — one identity moving, which is the whole reason SwiftUI has the modifier. No glass required: a tinted capsule that moves reads as native; a glass one that jump-cuts does not.

And the ✓ becomes a Gauge. A tick says something happened; an accessoryLinearCapacity gauge says how much, in exactly the same pixels, and reads its own value to VoiceOver. Seven days of gauges is a training week at a glance — which is the only reason the strip exists.

Log a sport stays, everywhere. It isn't an overflow item — it is a different job from the structured logger: a session you start (live, now, sets and reps), a sport you log (it already happened and you're telling the app about it). Bury it in a menu and a hybrid athlete's most common Sunday-evening action costs two taps and a guess.

The fault was never that both exist. It's that they're drawn at the same weight in two different vocabularies: a glowing full-bleed pill, then a hairline, then a whispered caption, then a dashed square with a lime word. Four devices spent saying "and also this" — and a dashed border is a web affordance that appears nowhere in iOS.

SwiftUI settles it with two button styles on one baseline: borderedProminent and bordered, each wrapping a Label so the symbol and the word are one unit. Nothing custom, nothing glass, and the hierarchy is legible in a tenth of a second.

The state change is then a fill change rather than a layout change: nothing logged, the primary wears the chartreuse; once the day holds training the accent retires and the two settle as equal bordered buttons. The card stops asking without anything moving.

The delta
  • −done-floor.tsx:145-152 — the dashed ＋ tile. The action survives; its box doesn't.
  • +AActionPair in kit.tsx: prominent + bordered, driven by one state prop covering all seven day states.
  • +ContentUnavailableView (native, @expo/ui) with systemImage, title and description.
  • −w.home.today.alsoTodaySubEmpty. The button is labelled, so the invitation is said once — in the description line.
House rules kept The no-bordered-box rule governs exits — an end-of-thing affordance carrying no content. This is an action, and a capsule is its native form. The rule that binds here is the other one: chartreuse stays reserved for the single primary, which is exactly what the secondary style protects.

sessionIcon(s) returns an emoji, set at 18 pt inside a tinted square. Three structural problems: an emoji can't take the row's colour (so the tile has to be tinted around it to carry meaning), it can't take a weight or optical size, and Apple redraws the set every couple of releases — the app's icon language changes underneath it without a commit.

A native Label takes systemImage and a title as one unit, aligns them the way every other iOS row aligns them, and inherits Dynamic Type. The kit already exports the SFSymbol type and uses symbols for chrome — arrow.left, ellipsis, timer — but never for content.

Then the row gets what it has never had: editing. SwipeActions is a real view in the package, so Edit and Delete arrive on the standard gesture, full-swipe included. Today the only way to fix a mis-logged session is to open it, and the only way to delete one is not from here at all. That's already on the backlog as swipe-actions-native — this is where it lands first.

The delta
  • +sessionSymbol(s): SFSymbol beside sessionIcon in core — one map, emoji kept as the off-iOS fallback.
  • +SwipeActions on each row: Edit (blue), Delete (terracotta, behind ConfirmationDialog).
  • +symbolEffect('bounce') fires once when a session lands on the day you're looking at.
  • −done-floor.tsx:116-118 — the 40×40 emoji tile.

Look at the lower third of the current screen: "Train your way" and the plan cards are smeared — two layers of text ghosting through a hand-rolled blur while the tab bar floats over them. iOS 26 solves it in one modifier: the soft scroll edge effect computes its fade against whatever is actually passing under the bar.

At the top, the same trade. Today and the date are static text that scrolls away, so the header keeps its full height forever. A large title that condenses into the bar on scroll — wordmark, streak and day in one 44 pt lockup — gives the section back roughly 120 pt of first-screen space. On a 6.1 inch phone that is the difference between the day's receipt being visible on load and needing a scroll.

Today the day changes by tapping a chip, and the detail remounts under a key={sel.dateKey}. Every figure hard-cuts, nothing says which direction you moved, and nothing connects the number that left with the number that arrived.

TabView with tabViewStyle({ type: 'page' }) is exactly this control, and it is in the package. Each day is a page; the rail above binds to the same selection through scrollPosition, so the two are one value rather than two states that can disagree. A haptic detent lands on each page.

Then the figures do what SwiftUI figures do: contentTransition('numericText') rolls 4.11 → 12.40 digit by digit in the direction of travel, with monospacedDigit keeping the column still while it happens.

This is the one place to spend motion. Everything else here gets quieter; the number earns the exception because the number is what the card is for. The backlog already asks for it — mobile-stat-tile, "so its figures can roll too".

The delta
  • −logbook-rail.tsx:123 — the key={sel.dateKey} remount, which is what makes the change a cut.
  • +ARollingFigure: native contentTransition on iOS, a Reanimated digit column elsewhere, one API.
  • +haptic("selection") per page, honouring useReducedMotion() exactly as liquid-seg already does.
Watch out Figures must keep coming through deviceTrueSession(). A rolling number computed from display-rounded values would animate to a figure the device panel beside it contradicts.

Right now a quick log always saves at "now", so the done floor hides its log row whenever you're looking at any day but today. That is a reasonable response to a real constraint — and it means the card cannot answer the most ordinary request an athlete has: I played on Saturday and forgot to log it.

A native DatePicker in the sheet fixes it, and it fixes it in the direction the screen is already pointing: the sheet opens dated to the day you were looking at. Scrub to Saturday, tap Log a sport, and the sheet is already Saturday's — you change nothing. The one place the app can be sure what day you mean is the day you're staring at.

The rest of the sheet comes from the same shelf: Form with Sections, Picker for the sport, Stepper for duration, TextField for distance. Roughly two hundred lines of hand-drawn field components come out.

Build order

three TestFlight builds
Build 1

01 + 03 + 04 — the still frame

Pure JS, shippable over EAS Update. The card becomes a Section, the two actions resolve into a primary and a secondary, the rows become Labels with swipe actions. Nothing animates yet and the section already reads native — this is the build that proves the direction on a device before any gesture work is paid for. It is also the one that puts Log a sport on the same baseline as the logger, which is worth watching in the numbers on its own.

Build 2

02 + 06 + 07 — the rail, the pager, the past

The week becomes a scrollable rail of gauges, the day becomes a TabView page, the figures roll, and the sport sheet takes a date. This build needs logbookDays(range) landed in core with tests before any UI touches it, and a device round for the paging/rail binding. It is where the three new capabilities arrive, so it is also where capabilities.ts gains its entries.

Build 3

05 — the screen around it

The large title and the scroll edge effect change native config, so they ride their own prebuild rather than sharing a build with the card work. One variable per build, per the Expo-module rule that already governs this repo.

Two constraints that outrank all seven Every figure on this card must keep reading through deviceTrueSession() — a redesign is exactly when a surface quietly starts drawing typed values again. And each move ships with its capabilities.ts entry in the same change; the mobile card is the golden standard on its own now, since the user-facing web client was retired in August.

Hybrid — Nutrition, add to meal

Six containers, one question.

The Snacks picker asks one thing — what did you eat? — and answers it with six containers of near-identical weight, two text fields that look alike, four tabs that hide three-quarters of your own food, and a screen that runs out of content at 40 % and leaves the rest black. Nothing on it is louder than anything else, so nothing on it is the subject.

Below: what the current screen is actually doing, then six concepts. Each takes one organizing idea all the way rather than tidying the six boxes, and each is drawn in the app's own vocabulary — Explore section heads, the rail-tail grammar, the sheet-pad rule, chartreuse reserved for go. Every one is specified for both clients; web and mobile ship together.

Nothing here removes a source or a way to log. Recent, Favorites, Meals and Foods all stay, and so does quick add. What every concept drops is the pill bar — the box drawn around those four, and the accent spent on which one is selected.

Everything is a box, so nothing is a subject.

The fixed points

Four sources stay. Quick add stays. The box goes.

Recent, Favorites, Meals and Foods are not four versions of one list to be pruned down — they are four different questions (what did I just eat, what do I always eat, what have I built, what have I saved), and quick add is the fastest path in the whole app. So none of the six concepts removes any of them.

What every concept removes is the treatment. The fault was never that four sources exist; it is that they are wrapped in a bordered, filled, radiused bar — a seventh container — and that the selected one wears chartreuse, the colour this app reserves for go. A tab is not a go. It is a statement about which list you are looking at, and it should be the quietest thing on the screen, not the loudest.

The replacement is one shared component, drawn once and used everywhere: the source line. Same four labels, same order, same job — selection carried by weight and a rule instead of a fill. No border, no radius, no tint, no accent. It costs one row of type and zero containers.

Two of the six don't switch at all: the Ledger and the Shelf show all four at once as section heads and as shelves, which is the stronger answer to the real complaint — that three-quarters of your own library is invisible at any moment. Both approaches keep the four; they differ on whether you pick a list or see them all.

Concept 01 — One Field — BUILT

One question, one field.

The grammar and the database are not two features. They are two answers to the same sentence.

Quick add already parses what you ate; search already looks it up. Splitting them into two identical boxes makes the athlete choose a mechanism before they've said a word. Here there is one field. You type, and every source answers underneath it in one ranked list: what the grammar understood first — the row you tap to approve, exactly as quick-add already works — then your own foods, then the world under a section head.

The barcode stays where it already is, as the field's trailing glyph, because scanning is the same question asked with a camera. At rest — nothing typed — the four sources sit under the field on the source line, and the screen has a floor before you touch it. Tap through them in the mock; all four are there, counted, with no box around them.

The thing that makes the merge worth it: once you type, the answer ranks across all four at once. Today you have to know which list a food is in before you can look for it — and the honest answer is usually "the one I'm not on". A saved meal, a favourite and an own-brand product compete in one ordered list, each labelled with where it came from.

Quick Log and Create Food fall to the tail of the list as bare + affordances. Per the exit rule, no ring: they grow the library in place, they don't go anywhere.

Concept 02 — What's Left — BUILT

The subject is the gap, not the box.

Nobody opens this screen to search. They open it because the day still owes them something.

So the screen states that first, in the app's display-weight mono, the way the Builder's One Number readout does: 612 kcal left, 48 g protein. Everything below is measured against it. Each row carries a delta — what it does to the gap — right-aligned in a tabular column, and the list is ordered by what closes this best, not by what you happened to log last March.

This is the concept that finally earns the accent. The chartreuse figure at the top is the one number on screen, it moves when you log, and the rings that move it are the only other accented things. Log something and watch the header fall — the screen answers, rather than waiting to be searched.

It also makes over-logging visible: a row that would push protein past target reads its delta in sand, not chartreuse, and the bar carries past the tick. No warning copy needed.

Quick add sits directly under the gap, and the source line under that — so the reading order is what you owe, say it, or pick it from one of your four lists. The ordering only rearranges the list you are on; it never moves a food between sources.

As built

Shipped on both clients. packages/core/src/nutrition-gap.ts computes the remainder once for both, and doing so retired two copies of the same 5 % tolerance that were living in the two clients under two different names — the hub's ring and this header can no longer disagree about whether you are over. Two decisions and one omission:

No target, no header
It renders nothing until there is an energy target to be short of. A header reading "2 000 left" against a number nobody set is the confident-wrong-number failure this app is built to avoid — the same reason the targets card asks for a weigh-in instead.
The word follows the sign, the colour follows the band
50 past a 2 000 target reads "50 kcal over" and is drawn calmly, because it is inside the tolerance. Two different questions; reading the word off the tolerance flag printed "50 kcal left" at 2 050 logged.
The re-ordering was NOT built
Deliberately. Recent is already ranked by the hour (04), and two rankings competing for one list is the drift this codebase keeps removing. What you actually eat at this hour beats what arithmetic says fits. The overshoot mark survives: a row that would take the day past target reads its figure line in sand.

Concept 03 — The Ledger

Delete every container.

A list of foods does not need boxes to be a list of foods.

The most restrained of the six, and the one closest to what the rest of the app already does well. No cards, no pill bar, no tinted tab, no fill of any kind: Explore-standard section heads (bold display title left, mono meta right), hairline rows, and one right-aligned tabular column of figures running the full height of the screen. Whitespace does the grouping that six borders were doing badly.

It doesn't switch sources — it shows all four. Recent, Favorites, Meals and Foods become the four section heads of one continuous column, in that order, each with its count on the right. Nothing is hidden behind a tap, and the source line isn't needed because the heads already say where you are as you scroll past them.

Because the figures line up in a real column, the screen becomes readable as a table — you can compare 133 against 63 against 190 at a glance, which is the actual question when you're standing in a kitchen at 21:12. The quick-add field sits above, unadorned, and the whole screen is legible in one look.

It is also the cheapest to build: it deletes code rather than adding it, and it is the natural home for the sources that already exist — a verified source is a mark beside the name, not another badge in another box.

Concept 04 — 21:12 — BUILT

The app knows the hour. Use it.

You do not eat a database at 21:12. You eat one of four things, and the app has watched you do it for months.

The screenshot is taken at 21:12 with the meal already set to Snacks, and the screen still opens as a blank search. This concept opens instead on what this athlete actually eats at this hour: a short list of large subjects, each carrying the portion it was last logged at, each one tap. No portion sheet for a food whose portion has never varied — that is a question the app already knows the answer to.

Nothing below it is lost — quick add and the four sources sit directly under the hour block, in the same order they have now. This concept only changes what greets you: the top of Recent, re-sorted by the clock, instead of an empty field. The hour list IS Recent; Favorites, Meals and Foods are one tap away on the source line, exactly as they are today.

The typographic move that makes it work: those rows are set at 18/700, bigger than any row elsewhere in the app. They are the content, not a list of candidates. And the dead 55 % becomes the screen's actual subject.

Concept 05 — The Tray

A snack is rarely one food.

The current screen commits on every tap and throws you back to the diary. Three foods means the same journey three times.

So let the picker accumulate. Tapping adds to a tray that sums live at the foot of the screen, and one commit writes the lot. The tray is the only filled surface on the screen, which is exactly right — it is the only thing holding a decision that hasn't happened yet.

It matches how the builder already treats a routine: compose, watch the number grow, save once. And it makes a mistake cheap — a wrong tap is a chip you flick off, not a diary entry you have to go and find.

It is also the concept that gets the most out of keeping four sources, because a tray survives switching between them: pull the yoghurt from Recent, the whey from Favorites and the oats from Meals, and commit once. Today each of those is a separate round trip through a separate tab.

The tray obeys the sheet rule exactly: its pad is sheetPadBottom(insetBottom), one number, a maximum and never a sum, and nothing inside it trails a pad of its own. It covers the tab bar rather than reserving clearance for it.

Not built — and why

This concept's premise was "the current screen commits on every tap and throws you back to the diary". That stopped being true when 01 and 04 shipped: a tap logs and the screen stays put, so three foods is now three taps with no round trips to undo. What the tray would still add — a running total before committing, and an undo before the fact — costs a second tap on every single-food log, which is the common case, to save one on the rarer multi-food case. That is the wrong way round.

The salvageable half is the undo: a strip of what you just logged, removable, without changing the one-tap commit. If the diary's own stepper and delete are not enough, that is the version worth drawing — not this one.

Concept 06 — The Shelf

Stop hiding three-quarters of the athlete's own food.

The four sources are exactly right. Making them exclusive is what's wrong: at any moment, 75 % of what this athlete has saved is one tap away and invisible.

Tabs are the right control when the lists are alternatives. These aren't — recents, favourites, saved meals and your own foods are all yours, and the answer is usually in whichever one you're not looking at. So all four stay and none of them hides: each becomes a shelf, one full-bleed rail per source, scrolling under the physical edge exactly as the exercise-widget rail does — negative margins the width of the gutter, matching internal padding, so resting cards still align with the content column.

Each rail ends in the shared rail tail, and the grammar is the one the rules already fix: a ringed arrow leaves — it opens that source as a full screen. It is never a bordered box, because a filled card at the end of a row of foods reads as one more food that turned out to be empty.

This is also the only concept that scales visibly with the athlete: the shelves get longer as the library grows, which is a quietly good feeling and something the tab bar could never express.

If you only build one thing

01 and 02 are the spine. The rest bolt on.

They were never six alternatives so much as one screen at six depths. One Field fixed the hierarchy, What's Left gave the screen a subject, and 21:12 gave it something to say before you type. Three are built. Of the rest, one was superseded by what shipped and two are the same question asked twice.

Built

01 One Field + 02 What's Left — shipped

The gap as the header, one input under it, the source line, then one ranked list. Five containers gone, the accent back to meaning go, the screen given both a subject and a floor — while keeping every source and every way to log.

Built

04 The Hour — shipped

What the list shows before you type. Pure gain — no new surface, no new schema, and it turns the empty 55 % into the fastest path in the app. 05 The Tray is the next one worth building.

Superseded

05 The Tray — not built

One-tap logging removed the round trips the tray existed to spare. It would now cost a tap on every single-food log to save one on the rarer multi-food case. See the note on the concept itself.

Style, not structure

03 The Ledger and 06 The Shelf

Two answers to the same question — how four sources are drawn when none of them hides. The Ledger stacks them as four heads in one column; the Shelf gives each a full-bleed rail. The Ledger is quieter and cheaper; the Shelf shows the library growing. Pick one, don't ship both.

What dies in all six
  • ✕The second text field — not quick add. The grammar stays and gets the whole field; what goes is the duplicate box beneath it.
  • ✕The pill BAR — not the four sources. Recent, Favorites, Meals and Foods all stay; the border, the fill, the radius and the chartreuse selected-state go. They become the source line, or four section heads, or four shelves.
  • ✕The instructional mono caption. The placeholder carries it, or the field is wrong.
  • ✕Card chrome on Quick Log and Create Food. They become bare + tails — they grow the library, they don't go anywhere.
  • ✕The black full-bleed result card. Rows sit in the content column on a hairline, like every other list in the app.
  • ✕The doubled chevron in the header. Back is ‹; the meal switcher keeps the ⌄.
